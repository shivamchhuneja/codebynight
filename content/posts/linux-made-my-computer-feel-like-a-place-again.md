I think macOS treats me a little like a kid.

It does a lot for me. It looks after the battery, hides most of the system stuff, gives me a nice clean place to work, and mostly makes sure I do not have to think too much about the computer itself. I use a Mac for work every day and I am very happy that it is good at this. Work exists, meetings exist, and I do not want to spend a Tuesday afternoon figuring out why my laptop has decided a file belongs to nobody now.

Still, after a while it can feel like I am borrowing the machine more than using it.

Then I open the Omarchy laptop to work through backend tutorials or get stuck on some Rust thing, and I get this slightly ridiculous feeling that I am an adult again.

Here I can change more and see more, and when I break something I at least have a chance of working out what I broke. The machine has opinions because Omarchy has plenty of them, but those opinions do not feel like a locked door. They feel more like somebody handed me a reasonably good room and said, "you can move things around if you want, just do not act surprised when you have to clean up afterwards."

Maybe that is why this laptop has started to feel like a place.

I wrote earlier about [using Omarchy as a learning OS](/posts/one-year-omarchy-learning-os-not-just-linux-setup/). That post was mostly about the environment I built around it: Rust, CodeCrafters, notes, papers, agents with limited access, and all the stuff I am trying to learn without accidentally turning it into another productivity obsession. This feels slightly different. The laptop has stopped being only a nice Linux machine that I own, and it feels more like somewhere I go to do a certain kind of work.

## macOS feels finished before I arrive

There is something genuinely great about a computer that mostly stays out of the way.

I open the Mac, work happens, and I can move through a normal day without thinking about package managers, services, config files, logs, or whether some process has decided to have a personal crisis in the background. That is useful. I am not trying to become one of those people who thinks every operating system needs to make you suffer a little before you are allowed to respect it.

But macOS often feels like it has already made the important choices for me.

Again, that is mostly the point. Most people do not need to care where a setting lives, what is running in the background, or why an app can access one folder but not another. The computer works, the email sends, the spreadsheet opens, and everyone gets on with their life.

I guess I have reached the annoying point where I want to know a little more now.

I am learning backend engineering. I am trying to understand systems stuff properly instead of only seeing the finished API or clean user interface. A lot of that learning starts with boring questions. Where is this file? What process is running? What owns this? Why did this tool behave differently after I changed one thing?

Linux keeps putting those questions in front of me.

## I can see the mess a bit more clearly here

Using Omarchy has not made me understand Linux deeply. I am still very capable of looking at an error, reading it three times, and somehow becoming less sure about what it means.

But the system feels less mysterious than it used to.

A package is a thing I installed. A config is somewhere I can find. A permission is not just a popup I click through because the app looks trustworthy enough. A weird hibernate problem is annoying, but it also makes me ask what is actually happening after the machine wakes up.

That does not mean every bit of friction is educational. Sometimes a fingerprint scanner is just being annoying and I would like it to stop. I have already written about that little problem in the earlier Omarchy post and my feelings have not become more philosophical since then.

The useful part is that Linux gives me somewhere to look.

On a more locked-down machine, I can feel the system deciding that this is not my problem. On this laptop, the answer is more like: it is your problem now, unfortunately, but the logs are probably around here somewhere.

That changes the relationship a little.

## Omarchy has enough opinions for me

The funny thing is that I do not want complete freedom either.

I have used Arch before. I have changed things, broken things, fixed things, spent way too long deciding whether some tiny visual change was making my desktop more beautiful or just making me avoid actual work. Linux can become a very elaborate way to feel technical while producing nothing apart from screenshots and a growing fear of updates.

Omarchy helps because it has already made enough decisions.

The desktop already has a shape and the tools fit together well enough that I do not need to build the entire environment from dust every time I want to learn something. It gives me a working place to begin, and then I can change the parts that start bothering me or the parts I genuinely want to understand.

That is a better deal for me right now.

I want to work through backend tutorials, get confused by a Rust interpreter project, read systems stuff, take notes, and occasionally try some strange local workflow. I do not need the operating system to become the main project every week.

At the same time, I like that it has not completely disappeared.

The machine still has texture because settings, services, files, packages, permissions, weird errors, and small choices are mine to make. I think that is why it feels more alive to me than a computer that has smoothed every edge away.

## A place is mostly made out of repeated things

I do not think a computer becomes personal because it has a wallpaper you like or because you changed the terminal theme.

Those things are nice. I like a good theme. I am not above caring about my desktop looking decent. I used Mac-like animations for a while on Omarchy, then removed them because apparently I enjoy recreating the operating system I am trying to get away from.

But the more important part is repetition.

I open this laptop when I want to learn something slowly, follow a tutorial without the work Slack tab sitting there like a small threat, or try a command and write down what I misunderstood before coming back to it later.

The machine has started collecting those habits, and that is probably what I mean by a place. It has a certain kind of work and attention attached to it, even when I am still not very good at the thing I am about to attempt.

## I like being allowed to grow into the machine

I do not need every computer to work this way.

My Mac is still the machine I trust to get through work. I like that it is predictable. I like that it usually handles the boring parts before I have a chance to create a problem for myself.

The Omarchy laptop gives me something else. It gives me room to become more capable around the machine, even if that mostly means I have become better at finding where the problem probably lives before asking AI to explain it to me.

That feels a little more adult than clicking through another helpful macOS popup and hoping I chose the right option.

Maybe I am projecting too much onto an operating system. That is possible. But I keep coming back to this laptop because it makes me feel closer to the work I am trying to do and the systems I am trying to understand.

Linux has made my computer feel like a place again. It is still a computer that breaks sometimes, and I still spend too long changing things that did not need changing. But it feels like mine in a way that is hard to explain without sounding slightly dramatic about a laptop.
